---
title: What the Machine Said
---

He started with the segment. With the carriage moved to its far stop and the type bars lifted a few at a time, he flooded each slot with spirits and watched the grey run out into the tray. It came out in threads at first, then in clouds, then — on the fourth pass — clear. He worked each bar until it fell back under its own weight, the way a door should close when you let it go.

By midnight the *e* moved. By one, the three bars that had locked together had made their peace and rose one at a time, each to its own place at the printing point. He did not oil the segment. He never did. Oil in a segment is a promise to the dust that it will be welcome.

## The test line

The test line is the same in every shop he has ever worked in, and it is not the one people expect. He rolled in a sheet of the cheap paper he keeps for the purpose and typed, slowly, every character on the keyboard in order, lower case and then upper, and then the sentence he has typed on some thousands of machines:

> Now is the time for all good men to come to the aid of the party.

The machine typed it cleanly. The *e*s were sharp. The spacing was even. Only the capital *W* sat a hair too high, and he made a note to adjust the shift in the morning.

He looked at the page for a long time. Then, because there was no one to see, he rolled the paper up one more line and typed a second sentence, the one the woman had not asked for but had come in to hear:

> It still remembers.

***

She came back on a Thursday. He had set the machine on the counter with a sheet of paper already in it, and he stepped away and let her type. She typed her father's name, and then her own, and then she stopped and listened to the carriage come home with its small, certain bell.

"That's it," she said. "That's the sound."

Elias nodded and wrote up the ticket, and did not charge her for the ribbon.
